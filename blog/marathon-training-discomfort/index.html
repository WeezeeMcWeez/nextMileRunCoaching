The Hard Truth About Marathon Training: Discomfort Is Part of the Process

By Corrinne Ward • August 5, 2026
← Back to Blog

One of the biggest misconceptions about marathon training is that, after a few weeks, it should start feeling easy. Many runners assume that if training feels hard, they’re doing something wrong. In reality, the opposite is often true.

Marathon training is supposed to challenge you.

That doesn’t mean every run should leave you exhausted or injured. It does mean that fatigue, self-doubt, sore legs, and difficult workouts are normal parts of the process. The runners who succeed aren’t necessarily the ones who avoid discomfort — they’re the ones who learn how to accept it, work through it, and keep moving forward.

Why Training Feels Hard

Running a marathon requires your body to adapt to increasing physical demands. Long runs, tempo workouts, hill repeats, and weekly mileage all place stress on the body so it can become stronger. If training never feels challenging, there may not be enough stimulus for improvement.

Legendary running coach Greg McMillan often emphasizes that breakthrough performances occur when athletes learn to stay engaged and focused during the difficult portions of training and racing, rather than backing away from the challenge.

The goal of training isn’t comfort. The goal is adaptation.

Your Brain Has a Bigger Role Than You Think

Most runners assume marathon success is all about strong legs and healthy lungs. Exercise science tells us there’s another important factor: perceived effort.

Research has shown that endurance performance is heavily influenced by how hard an effort feels. As fatigue accumulates, our perception of effort increases, and that sensation often becomes a limiting factor in performance.

Think about a long run when your watch says you’re maintaining pace, but every mile suddenly feels harder than the last. Often, what’s changing isn’t your physical capability — it’s your perception of the effort required.

This is why experienced runners spend so much time developing mental skills alongside physical fitness. They’re not trying to eliminate discomfort. They’re learning how to continue despite it.

Stop Interpreting Discomfort as Failure

Many runners fall into a common mental trap:

  • Hard workout = I’m out of shape.
  • Heavy legs = My training isn’t working.
  • Tough long run = I’m not ready for race day.

In reality, those experiences may indicate the exact opposite.

Fatigue is often evidence that your body is adapting to training stress. Challenging workouts provide opportunities to practice pacing, resilience, and confidence under pressure.

Of course, there is a difference between normal training discomfort and pain that could signal injury. Learning that distinction is important. But ordinary fatigue, soreness, and moments when you don’t feel motivated are expected parts of marathon preparation.

Confidence Isn’t Built by Positive Thinking

Many people believe confidence comes from motivational quotes or positive affirmations.

Research on self-efficacy suggests something different: confidence develops primarily through mastery experiences, or successfully overcoming challenges.

Every time you complete a difficult workout, finish a long run, or push through a day when you didn’t feel like training, you’re collecting evidence that you can handle adversity. That’s where real confidence comes from — not from telling yourself you’re capable, but from proving it.

By race day, you want to be able to look back and say:

“I’ve already done hard things. I can do this too.”

Mental Toughness Is Built, Not Born

Some runners seem mentally tough from the start, but research suggests mental toughness is largely developed through experience, exposure to challenge, and learning how to respond to adversity.

Every difficult training session becomes an opportunity to build that skill.

When you continue a run despite wanting to quit, you’re training mental endurance. When you get up early for a long run after a poor night’s sleep, you’re training mental endurance. When you stay committed to your training plan despite setbacks, you’re training mental endurance.

Just as muscles adapt to stress, your mindset adapts to stress as well.

A Better Question to Ask

Instead of asking, “Why does marathon training feel so hard?” try asking, “What is this hard run teaching me?”

Maybe it’s teaching patience. Maybe it’s teaching pacing. Maybe it’s teaching confidence. Maybe it’s teaching you that you’re stronger than you thought.

Every challenging run offers something valuable if you’re willing to look for it.

The Bottom Line

The next time a long run feels difficult or a workout leaves you questioning your fitness, remember this: marathon training is not supposed to feel comfortable.

Science shows that endurance performance depends not only on physical fitness but also on how we perceive and respond to effort. Learning to tolerate discomfort, managing fatigue, and building confidence through challenging experiences are all essential parts of becoming a stronger runner.

The hard days are not a sign that training is failing. They are often a sign that training is working.

The marathon doesn’t reward the runner who avoided discomfort. It rewards the runner who learned how to move through it.

Sources referenced: Frontiers in Psychology (frontiersin.org), RevSystems, Six Minute Mile, Jackson-Madison County Schools Center for Athletic Opportunity, and UCAN.
